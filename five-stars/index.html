FIVE STARS

Five stars for the delivery. What does the driver keep?

Directed by Corey Spangler · Built by AI

You are the app.

You run a food-delivery platform in a fictional city for one quarter (13 weeks). You are not a driver. Your one job, set by the board: hit the quarterly revenue target of ◇ scenario.

In this scenario drivers are independent contractors: they pay for their own cars, and they are paid per delivery, not for time spent waiting for the next order.

  • Take rate — your cut of the customer's delivery fee.
  • Base pay — what you pay the driver per delivery.
  • Quests & streaks — pressure on drivers to accept every offer and stay online.
  • Surge cap — how high fees can climb where orders pile up.

How to play

  • Drag the four levers at any time. The city reacts live.
  • Each dot is a group of drivers. Green, amber, red shows what they are netting per hour after vehicle costs. A hollow ring means waiting, unpaid.
  • Tap or click any dot to see its numbers.
  • Watch the two ledgers at the top: yours, and the drivers'.
  • Each week takes about 30 seconds. A quarter takes about 6½ minutes (or less at 2× / 4× speed).

    Part 3 of 4

    The documented evidence

    Only these real-world facts are used. Everything else in the game is scenario.

    • In California, app-based delivery drivers are classified as independent contractors under Proposition 22. Voters approved Prop 22 on November 3, 2020. It classifies app-based rideshare and delivery drivers as independent contractors rather than employees, so the state's AB 5 employment test does not apply to them.
      Sources: California Legislative Analyst's Office, Proposition 22 analysis (2020); Ballotpedia, California Proposition 22 (2020).
    • That classification has held. On July 25, 2024, the California Supreme Court unanimously upheld Prop 22 in Castellanos v. State of California.
      Sources: Justia, Law Classifying Gig Workers as Contractors Upheld in California Supreme Court (2024); Perkins Coie, California Supreme Court Upholds Proposition 22 (2024).
    • As contractors, drivers carry vehicle costs and unpaid waiting time. The LAO's analysis describes drivers using their own cars and paying their own expenses. Prop 22 did add an earnings floor and some benefits; the LAO notes the floor is based on time spent driving, not time spent waiting for a request.
      Source: California Legislative Analyst's Office, Proposition 22 analysis (2020).

    What the game does not claim: it does not reproduce any real company's pay formula, take rate, driver earnings, or revenue. Classification rules outside California differ.

    Part 4 of 4

    The conclusion the author draws

    This is Corey Spangler's reading, not a finding of the simulation.

    Nobody in this game is a villain, including you. You were handed one number and four levers. In this city, the levers that moved the number most were the ones that moved costs onto drivers: a bigger cut, less base pay, more pressure to accept, more unpaid miles chasing surge. The drivers who stayed red were the ones the scenario gives the fewest other options. When the people doing the work carry the car and the waiting, a platform's revenue target points at them, whoever happens to be running it. Who carries those costs is a policy choice, and policy choices can be made differently.

    Disagree? Good. Run the quarter again, try to hit the target without turning the map red, and tell us which assumption you think is wrong and why.
