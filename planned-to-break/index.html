Planned to Break

A playable argument about planned obsolescence — and the company that makes perfect products.

You are the CEO of the Meridian Home Goods Company ○ fictional, across three product generations: light bulbs, washing machines, then phones. Each generation, you set three things: how long the product is designed to last, what it costs, and how long you warranty it.

Here is the whole trap, up front. A product that lasts is a product nobody has to replace. Your owners' repeat purchases — the engine of your growth — are a function of how fast the installed base dies. Build it to last and your customers love you while your sales flatten and the board loses patience. Build it to fail and the growth is beautiful, right up until the teardown video.

Two ledgers, kept separately, never merged:

  • Company Value — cumulative profit, in dollars. This is what the board watches.
  • Reputation — what your customers think of you. This is what the internet watches.

The board also judges each generation's unit sales against a target set by the standard replacement cycle ◇ assumption — the industry's normal lifespan, not yours. Beat the target and patience grows. Miss it and patience drains. Run out of patience and you are replaced, whatever your reputation says.

All economy figures in this game are ◇ scenario assumptions. Sales figures are ＝ calculated from the installed-base model printed in the debrief. The two historical facts in the debrief are ◆ sourced. Nothing else here claims to be real.
