The Blank Slate: Why We Are Taking Back Our Parties From the Inside Out

By Corey Spangler[cite: 1]

I've been thinking a lot about why good people run away from public service[cite: 1, 2].

Whenever I try to recruit folks around here to run for school board or city council (people who run small businesses, trade guys, working moms who know how to manage a budget) they always hit me with the exact same line:

"I'm just not political, Corey. I'm not a politician. That stuff is toxic."[cite: 1, 2]

They're completely right[cite: 1, 2]. What happens in our capitol buildings and on cable news isn't stewardship[cite: 2]. It isn't applied problem-solving[cite: 2]. It's just doing politics[cite: 2]. And doing politics is the whole problem[cite: 2].

I'm in my second term on the Cabot City Council[cite: 1, 2]. I chair the Lonoke County Democratic Committee, and I sit on the State Committee of the Democratic Party of Arkansas[cite: 1, 2]. It still feels weird when someone calls me a politician[cite: 2].

I don't think of myself that way[cite: 2]. I was reading Slavoj Žižek's Against Progress a while back, and it hit me that I'm basically just a grad student who got ahold of a little bit of local authority[cite: 2]. I'm just trying to figure out how to use it so working-class people don't keep getting run over[cite: 2].

A few years ago, I came across the story of the original Stoic Opposition in Rome[cite: 2]. They were a group of philosophers around Emperor Nero who didn't launch a doomed civil war[cite: 2]. Their entire legacy came down to one simple, dangerous stance: they just refused to go along with the bullshit[cite: 2].

For a while, I thought maybe our project should just be pure pragmatism: tell folks to register with whatever party is in power where they live, infiltrate it, and take it over.

I was wrong.

Telling people to be political chameleons is fundamentally dishonest. It asks you to lie about who you are, and your neighbors will smell that from a mile away.

Even if we purge every dime of corporate PAC cash from this country tomorrow, we are still going to have real disagreements about economics, zoning, and how we live together. That's fine. What isn't fine is that both parties have been hollowed out into marketing shells that take money from whoever will give it and leave regular people in the dirt[cite: 1, 2].

We don't need opportunists. We need people who will go into the party that actually speaks to them and do the unglamorous administrative work to fix it from the bottom up[cite: 2].

And here's the thing almost nobody realizes: the machinery is basically undefended.

Local party committees across this country are ghost towns. Precinct seats sit empty year after year.

Most people see an empty room and think it's hopeless. I look at an empty room and see a blank slate.

When a room is empty, you don't have to spend a fortune fighting an entrenched machine. You just have to show up with a few friends.

That's what we did in Lonoke County[cite: 2]. We had a blank slate, so we swung for the fences[cite: 2]. We drafted, introduced, and passed a binding rule that bans corporate PAC money from our county party[cite: 2]. We didn't ask Washington for permission[cite: 2]. We just used the rules that were already on the books[cite: 2].

This project, The Stoic Opposition, is here to help you do the exact same thing in your own county[cite: 2].

Pick the party you actually believe in. Find the empty seats. Walk in, take the chairs, and pass the rule[cite: 2].

We're better than this current mess[cite: 1]. Let's get to work.
