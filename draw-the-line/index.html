DRAW THE LINE

58% of the votes. 40% of the seats. Same voters — your pencil decides.
This is Meridian County — fictional, 25 precincts, 100 voters each. Countywide, BLUE has 1,460 voters (58.4%) and RED has 1,040 (41.6%). The county elects a 5-seat board, one seat per district, and every district must hold exactly 5 connected precincts. Nobody's vote changes in this game. Only the lines move. Mode A: The Mission. Mode B: Draw It Proportional.

Mode B unlocks after you complete the Mission. The debrief unlocks after both maps exist — because the debrief is the comparison.

🗺️ The board

Each precinct shows its BLUE / RED voters. BLUE voters are the majority almost everywhere you look — except a few precincts where RED runs strong. Geography is the whole game.

✏️ Drawing

Pick a district (1–5), then click precincts to paint them into it. Click a precinct in the selected district to unpaint it. A district needs exactly 5 precincts, all touching (up / down / left / right — diagonals don't count).

🏆 Winning a district

A district's seat goes to whoever has more of its 500 voters. Win the precincts' voters, not their land — a district won 251–249 counts exactly the same as one won 490–10. That sentence is the entire scandal.

Fictional county only. The parties are named BLUE and RED because the mechanism does not care what either of them believes — whichever side holds the pencil can do this, and in real map rooms, both have. No data collected, no external calls.

The debrief: the mission was the difference

Look at what just happened, because you did all of it. The same hands — yours — drew both maps, from the same 2,500 voters, who never moved and never changed their minds. One map gave 41.6% of the voters 60% of the seats. The other gave the seats out roughly the way the votes were cast. The voters were not the variable. The mission was.

That is why "just draw fair lines" is not a talent question. There is no shortage of people who *can* draw a fair map — you drew one in Mode B, probably in a few minutes. The question is who is in the room, holding the pencil, with what instructions, when the lines get drawn. A map-drawer hired to protect an incumbent will protect the incumbent. A map-drawer bound by rules — compactness, proportionality, public criteria set before anyone sees the partisan math — draws a different map with the same hands.

So the fight is never really about the lines. It is about the room where the lines get voted on: the redistricting commission, the legislature, the county board — and who showed up to those rooms in the years before the census, when nobody was paying attention. Whoever shows up to the rooms where the lines are drawn wins before a single vote is cast. The seats were decided by attendance first, and by voters second.

The committee room is still empty, most places. The map room is too. Show up and take the chairs — before the next lines are drawn, not after.
