The Lonoke County Blueprint

What Happens When You Show Up to an Empty Room

Have y'all ever actually been to a county committee meeting on a Tuesday night?[cite: 1]

Most people think party politics is run like a spy movie, with powerful operatives in smoke-filled rooms deciding everything.

It really isn't. Most of the time it's six people sitting on metal folding chairs in a public library basement, arguing about what kind of sheet cake to buy for an annual dinner.

In Lonoke County, we realized that kind of low attendance isn't a problem[cite: 2]. It's an open door[cite: 2].

If a county committee has forty seats on paper, but only six people show up to the meetings, four people are an absolute majority. That's just basic math. And under almost every state's party rules, if a precinct seat is empty, the folks in the room can vote to fill that vacancy right then and there.

We had an empty room in Lonoke County, so we didn't waste our breath passing meaningless resolutions about national news stories[cite: 2]. We dealt with what was right in front of us: our own committee, our own bank account, and our own integrity[cite: 2].

We brought an amendment to our county bylaws. It did two simple things:

  1. It defined corporate PACs and lobbyists in plain English[cite: 2].
  2. It banned our county committee from taking their money or endorsing candidates who take it[cite: 2].

Because the room was a blank slate, there wasn't a room full of corporate lobbyists sitting there to stop us[cite: 2]. We made the case that working people will never trust a party that takes corporate checks, we called the vote, and it passed[cite: 2].

George Washington warned us in his farewell address about parties becoming tools for unprincipled men to subvert the power of the people. He was dead right. But they only get away with it when normal people stay home.

Stop waiting on somebody from the top of the ticket to come save your county[cite: 2]. They aren't coming. Walk into the empty room and take the gavel yourself.
