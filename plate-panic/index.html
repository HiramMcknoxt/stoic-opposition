🔬 Prove It — run real OCR, right here

No trick code, and no downloads — the first version of this panel borrowed the Tesseract OCR engine from a CDN, and if that CDN is blocked the panel dies, which rather proves the wrong point. So this version carries its own OCR engine inside this one file: a template-matching reader written in plain JavaScript (the classic OCR technique — segment the serial band into characters, then match each character's pixels against rendered templates). It generates a brand-new plate drawn to look like a real Arkansas plate — sky-blue-to-white gradient, the Crater of Diamonds diamond in the center, maroon “Arkansas” on top, “The Natural State” at the bottom, current ABC 12D format (Arkansas skips O and Q — so does the generator) — with an invented serial, because publishing real plate data is exactly what this game is against. Then the built-in engine reads it. No server, no CDN, no upload. It works offline, from a file, forever.



Press Run Real OCR. The engine is already in this file — nothing downloads, nothing loads from a CDN, and it works with the Wi-Fi off.

Honest caveat: this plate is flat, clean, and straight-on — the easy case, even with the diamond behind the serial and the real Arkansas layout. Real roads add angle, night, dirt, and speed — which is an argument about cameras, placement, and confidence thresholds, not about paying rent forever on plate-reading software. Production open-source stacks (OpenALPR, FastALPR, CodeProject.AI) use plate-trained models and run locally for $0 in license fees.

🛡️ Breach Boss — the security round

🚔 Verify It — the stop decision

The camera says HIT. Papers, please. Plate readers match numbers — they misread characters, and they are notoriously sloppy about which state issued the plate. The alert in front of you is a lead, not a fact. Your whole job is the five-second check: state, every character, vehicle. About a quarter of these alerts carry the right number on the wrong state's plate.

The receipts: Aurora, Colorado, August 2020 — Brittney Gilliam and four children were held at gunpoint, face-down on the pavement, because a plate reader matched her Colorado SUV's number to a stolen Montana motorcycle. Wrong state, wrong vehicle, right number. Aurora settled for $1.9 million. The Institute for Justice has documented at least 27 erroneous plate-reader encounters since 2018 — and attributes most of them to human error, not camera error. Independent testing (IPVM) has found readers misidentify a plate's state roughly one time in ten. Every one of those failures was preventable by the check you just did: state, characters, vehicle — before the stop, not after.

🔎 The Auditor — the Citizens Commission's actual job

Every search of the plate database leaves a log line. Somebody is supposed to read them. Today, that's you. Thirteen searches from one small department. Approve the legitimate ones. Flag the abuse. This is not a hypothetical problem — misuse of plate databases by officers is one of the most documented failure modes of these systems, and the only thing standing against it is a person reading this log.

🧹 Stale Hit — the list is a living thing

A car gets reported stolen and goes on the hotlist. Then it gets recovered — and somebody has to take it off the list. That somebody is you, and the paperwork never stops. Clear the RECOVERED cars before a patrol unit finds them first and makes a "stolen car" stop on the owner. Leave the STILL STOLEN cars alone — pulling a live one off the list is its own disaster. Sixty seconds. The queue is not sorry.

Stale data is a documented cause of wrongful plate-reader stops — recovered vehicles that were never cleared have put innocent owners on the pavement in the Institute for Justice's case files. The software didn't fail in those stories. The list did. And lists are maintained by people, on purpose, or not at all.

🏘️ Two Towns — five years, two choices

Same town, same nine cameras, same stolen-car problem. Leaseville rents the whole system from a vendor, the way most cities do it. Ownburg buys its cameras, keeps its data, and stands up a Citizens Commission. You make Ownburg's calls, one a year. Leaseville's are made for it — that's rather the point. Safety comes out about the same in both towns, because the cameras are the same cameras. Everything else is governance.
