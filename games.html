The Stoic Opposition Presents

Systems You Can Play.

An educational video game suite.

Every game on this shelf makes one argument by handing you the controls. You do not play the hero. You play the operator — the candidate, the billing stack, the store manager, the line-drawer — and you find out what the system pays for and what it punishes, by trying to win it.

Why does a project about county party committees publish games about hospital bills and store schedules? Because the same pattern runs through all of them: an incentive structure that pays for the bad outcome, protected by party machinery that takes money from the people who profit from it. Unbought is the trap and the exit. The other four are systems that stay broken while the rooms stay bought.

House rules for this shelf

Systems, not villains. No game on this shelf has an evil character. Every actor is responding to incentives you can see. The argument is with the structure and its arithmetic.

No unlabelled numbers. Figures in these games come in four kinds, labelled where they appear: sourced (a named source, in the game’s credits), assumed (a scenario parameter you can inspect), calculated (arithmetic on the game’s own figures), and fictional (invented for the scenario, like the county in Draw The Line).

The debrief keeps four things separate. Each game ends with one screen: the simulation’s assumptions, your results, the documented evidence, and the conclusion the author draws. You are invited to disagree with the fourth.

Free, and yours. Every game is a single file that runs in a browser. The games send no gameplay data and use no analytics; this site is hosted on GitHub Pages, which processes ordinary request information, as any host does. Copy them, fork them, put them on your own site. The point is the argument, not the traffic.
