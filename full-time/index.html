The subsidy is not a side effect of the business model. It is the business model.
Two games in one, about the same store. In Mode A — The Manager, you run a big-box store for 8 weeks against a weekly profit target set by people above you. In Mode B — The Cliff, you are one of that store's workers for 12 months, trying to work your way off public benefits. Play A first. Then play B, and watch where Mode A's savings came from. Nobody in this game is a villain. Everybody is doing arithmetic someone else set up.


🏬 Mode A — The Manager

Set the hours cap, the wage, and the schedule for 60 workers. Hit the weekly profit target and earn your bonus. A second ledger — quieter than the first — keeps a different total.

🧾 Mode B — The Cliff

One worker, two kids, one store job. Take the extra shifts. Take the raise. Take the second job. Watch what happens to your total resources when the benefits phase out faster than the pay phases in.

🎯 The goal you'll chase

Self-sufficiency: a month where your earnings alone cover the household budget and you receive no benefits at all. It is possible in Mode B. Count what it costs to get there.

Scenario, sources & assumptions

Numbers discipline (series design principle 4): this game presents no invented statistic as real. It contains no real company, no real store, and no claim about any actual employer's workforce. What it contains is one fully disclosed fictional-but-typical scenario — a model, printed in full below, built on the standard structure of U.S. food assistance, childcare subsidy, Medicaid-style coverage, and housing help — programs whose amounts and thresholds genuinely vary by state and year, which is why the game refuses to quote yours.

The scenario (all figures are this game's assumptions)

Mode A store: 60 workers · gross margin before labour $58,000/wk · other operating costs $18,000/wk · profit target $12,000/wk · manager bonus $750 per target week · benefits loading $0/wk per worker under a 35-hr cap, $55 at 35 hrs, $110 at 40 hrs · erratic scheduling saves the store $900/wk in lean-staffing flexibility; stable scheduling costs $100/wk in slack coverage. Subsidy Ledger model, per worker per month: food assistance = 22% of the gap between monthly earnings and $1,500 (max $280); health support = $310 if the job provides no benefits and earnings are under $1,900 ($120 if earnings are higher, $140 at a 35-hr benefits tier, $40 at a full 40-hr benefits tier); housing help = $180 under $1,300/mo earnings, $70 under $1,700; erratic schedules add $60 (extra paid care and compliance churn). The ledger converts at 60 workers, 4.33 weeks/month. "Labour savings" are measured against the game's own baseline: 40 hrs at the living-wage tier with full benefits.

Mode B worker: single parent, two children · budget $2,650/mo as itemised in Mode B (rent can rise mid-year — watch Month 7) · childcare costs $520/mo · food assistance: $520/mo at earnings ≤ $900, phasing out 30¢ per $1 above $900 · childcare subsidy: $380/mo while earnings ≤ $1,650, then zero — a hard cliff, as many real subsidy schedules have been · housing help: $200/mo at earnings ≤ $1,400, phasing out to $0 at $1,800 · health coverage: full coverage valued at $280/mo while earnings ≤ $1,900; above that, coverage is lost and a $185/mo premium becomes a work cost · transport $140/mo, +$30 above 35 hrs/wk, +$70 with a second job, which also adds $60/mo of extra care costs and pays $260/mo. One-off life events (car repair, a sick kid, a store hours cut) are the game's, dated inside the fiction.

What is real: the structure — hours caps held just under benefits thresholds, phase-outs that can outrun a raise, subsidy cliffs, and public programs quietly completing a low-wage payroll — is the documented shape of these systems, and the reason every figure here is labelled a scenario instead of a statistic. For your own state's current amounts, your state benefit agency's published tables are the source; this game is the argument, not the table.
