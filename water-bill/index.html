THE WATER BILL

The check clears this year. The pipes come due later.

Directed by Corey Spangler · Built by AI

Two acts, one water system.

Act 1 — you are the mayor of Cedar Fork, a fictional town of about 20,000 people. The budget has a hole ◇ scenario. A private operator offers a large payment up front to lease the town's water system for 30 years. Your job: close the hole.

Act 2 — 30 years in fast-forward. If you signed, you switch chairs and run the system as the operator, with investors expecting a set return. If you refused, you stay mayor and run it as a public utility. Either way, the main lever is maintenance.

How to play

  • The town is drawn in 3D-style isometric view. Pipes run under the streets: blue is sound, amber is aging, red is cracked and leaking.
  • Act 1: choose one of three options. Refusing opens a short budget puzzle.
  • Act 2: drag the maintenance lever while the years roll by (about 5 seconds a year; 2× and 4× available). Watch for main breaks and boil-water notices.
  • Two ledgers stay on screen: yours, and what residents pay and drink.
  • About 5–8 minutes.

    Part 3 of 4

    The documented evidence

    Only these real-world facts are used. Everything else in the game is scenario.

    • Long-term leases of public water systems, with rates set by contract formula, are a real U.S. municipal pattern. In December 2012, the Bayonne, New Jersey, Municipal Utilities Authority signed a 40-year water and wastewater concession with a joint venture of United Water (part of Suez) and the investment firm KKR. The authority kept ownership; rates were to follow a formula in the agreement. The deal was described as paying off more than $130 million of Bayonne's debt.
      Sources: KKR, United Water and KKR Sign Unique Utility Partnership with City of Bayonne, NJ (Dec. 20, 2012); Knowledge at Wharton, A Tale of Two Public-Private Partnership Cities (2015).
    • Its scheduled rate increases were widely reported. Water and sewer bills rose 8.5% in 2012, with further increases in later years. Local reporting explained that when revenue fell below the contract's “revenue path,” rates rose to make up the difference, and by 2016 rates were reported to have risen almost 28% since the deal.
      Sources: Knowledge at Wharton (above); Hudson County View, reporting on Bayonne's annual water rate increase and the revenue path; NJTV News via EnviroPolitics, report on Bayonne water rates (2016).
    • The deal has defenders. A Federal Highway Administration case study credits the concession with a $150 million up-front payment that retired the city's debts, plus system upgrades including advanced metering; the operator has said later increases ran about 5% a year, below the national average.
      Sources: U.S. Federal Highway Administration, Bayonne, New Jersey case study; Hudson County View, report on calls for a state review of the deal, with the operator's response.

    What the game does not claim: Cedar Fork and Clearwell are invented. Their contract terms, rates and pipe conditions are not Bayonne's, and nothing here says Bayonne's pipes were neglected.

    Part 4 of 4

    The conclusion the author draws

    This is Corey Spangler's reading, not a finding of the simulation.

    Nobody in Cedar Fork wanted bad water. The mayor had a real hole and a real offer. The operator had investors and a contract that guaranteed revenue but not pipes. When what residents pay is fixed by schedule and upkeep is the one cost left to move, deferring maintenance is how the return gets made, and the bill for that deferral arrives years later, to people who never signed anything. The question isn't public versus private. It's whether a contract ties what residents pay to what the system actually gets.

    Disagree? Good. Play it again: sign with caps, refuse and raise taxes, or run the operator at full maintenance, and tell us which assumption you think is wrong and why.
